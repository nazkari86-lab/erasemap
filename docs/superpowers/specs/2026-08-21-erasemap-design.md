# EraseMap Research and System Design

Date: 2026-08-21

Status: proposed design approved in conversation; awaiting review of this written specification

## 1. Summary

EraseMap is a reproducible research prototype for detecting incomplete erasure of a person's biometric data across a controlled digital system. It models raw records, derived biometric templates, indexes, backups, and trained models as a typed provenance graph. After an erasure request, EraseMap finds any residual path from the person's identity to an active processing purpose, explains the shortest counterexample, and computes a low-cost valid remediation plan.

The project is not a legal-compliance engine and does not claim to audit the real eGov platform, Apple Face ID, or any production government system. Its scientific contribution will be tested on synthetic government-style architectures and open or synthetic biometric representations.

## 2. Plain-language explanation

Deleting one photograph does not necessarily delete every copy or result made from it. A face template, search index, backup, or trained model may still contain a usable trace. EraseMap draws a map of every registered path taken by the person's data and refuses to say "complete" while any prohibited path remains active.

## 3. Research question and hypotheses

Primary research question:

> Does a typed provenance-graph audit reduce false declarations of complete biometric erasure compared with a flat checklist, a signed receipt without semantic checks, and untyped descendant traversal?

Primary hypothesis, H1:

> EraseMap will have a lower false-complete rate than every baseline on unseen system topologies with injected residual traces.

Secondary hypotheses:

- H2: typed evidence validation will detect more invalid or stale deletion proofs than untyped graph traversal.
- H3: a cost-aware remediation planner will produce lower-cost valid plans than delete-all while covering every registered prohibited artifact.
- H4: the paired EraseTwin model audit will agree with exact retraining more reliably than comparison against randomly selected nonmembers.

The project remains valid if any hypothesis is rejected. Negative and null results will be retained.

## 4. Scope

### In scope

- Controlled, instrumented digital architectures generated by the benchmark.
- Raw biometric-like records, templates or embeddings, indexes, caches, backups, and small classification or verification models.
- Erasure and restriction-of-processing policies represented as machine-readable constraints.
- Detection of residual paths within the registered provenance graph.
- Typed evidence checks, tamper-evident receipts, and remediation planning.
- Synthetic identities and datasets whose licenses permit research use.
- Reproducible experiments on a local computer.

### Out of scope

- Claims about data copies outside the instrumented graph.
- Physical proof that arbitrary storage media have been irreversibly destroyed.
- Legal advice or automatic judgments of legal compliance.
- Auditing eGov, banks, Apple Secure Enclave, or other real services without written authorization and access.
- Collection of classmates' faces, voices, government identifiers, or other new human-participant biometric data.
- Production deployment in a high-stakes identity system.

## 5. Threat model and claim boundary

EraseMap assumes that every in-scope creation, copy, transformation, model-training use, and deletion action is registered by trusted instrumentation. The system may face stale proofs, altered receipts, replayed receipts, incorrect artifact states, missed cleanup actions, and deliberately injected residual nodes inside that registered environment.

EraseMap does not defend against an operator that bypasses all instrumentation and creates an unknown external copy. A green result means:

> Every in-scope descendant registered in the committed provenance graph is covered by evidence valid for its artifact type and policy state.

It does not mean:

> No copy exists anywhere in the world.

## 6. System model

The provenance graph is a directed graph `G = (V, E)`.

Each node has:

- pseudonymous subject identifier;
- artifact identifier and type;
- processing purpose;
- storage or model location;
- creation and retention timestamps;
- legal or policy state;
- current lifecycle state;
- commitment hash;
- evidence reference.

Initial artifact types:

- `SOURCE_RECORD`
- `BIOMETRIC_TEMPLATE`
- `SEARCH_INDEX_ENTRY`
- `CACHE_ENTRY`
- `BACKUP_COPY`
- `MODEL_INFLUENCE`
- `AUDIT_RECEIPT`

Edge types:

- `COPIED_TO`
- `DERIVED_INTO`
- `INDEXED_AS`
- `BACKED_UP_AS`
- `USED_TO_TRAIN`
- `SUPERSEDED_BY`

Lifecycle states:

- `ACTIVE`
- `ERASED`
- `BLOCKED`
- `WAITING_EXPIRY`
- `UNVERIFIED`

## 7. Core algorithms

### 7.1 Residual-path detection

Starting from a subject's source nodes, the auditor traverses all registered descendants. A path is residual when it terminates in an active processing sink and is not interrupted by a policy-valid erased or blocked state.

The result includes:

- complete set of residual nodes;
- shortest residual counterexample path;
- reason each node failed evidence validation;
- honest overall state: `COMPLETE`, `INCOMPLETE`, or `UNVERIFIED`.

`UNVERIFIED` is distinct from failure and success. Missing evidence must never be silently treated as erasure.

### 7.2 Typed evidence validation

Each artifact type has its own validation contract:

- records and index entries: committed pre-deletion identifier plus absence in the controlled store;
- templates: absence under the committed template identifier and subject mapping;
- caches: invalidation record plus absence after the declared propagation window;
- backups: scheduled expiry or cryptographic-erasure evidence; pending backups remain `WAITING_EXPIRY`;
- models: EraseTwin behavioral audit against exact retraining in the experimental setting;
- receipts: signature, timestamp, request binding, graph-root binding, and anti-replay checks.

A valid signature proves integrity and attribution of a statement, not the truth of the deletion claim. Semantic evidence validation remains mandatory.

### 7.3 Cost-aware remediation planning

Each available remediation action has:

- monetary or normalized compute cost;
- execution time;
- artifact coverage;
- permitted policy states;
- expected evidence type.

The planner selects actions that cover all required residual artifacts while respecting non-deletable retention constraints. The initial exact solver will use integer programming for benchmark-sized graphs. A deterministic greedy approximation will provide a scalable baseline.

Restriction of processing and physical erasure are never treated as equivalent. If policy permits only blocking, the final state reports `BLOCKED`, not `ERASED`.

### 7.4 EraseTwin model audit

For an identity-like forget item `f`, matching selects a guaranteed nonmember twin `t` using features independent of the audited model's membership output. Matching controls class or identity role, input quality, acquisition conditions, and baseline difficulty.

The unlearned model `M_U` is compared with an exact retraining reference `M_R`. The initial paired gap is:

`ETG = abs((s(M_U, f) - s(M_U, t)) - (s(M_R, f) - s(M_R, t)))`

The score function `s` and pass threshold will be frozen before holdout evaluation. Random nonmember, same-class nonmember, and embedding-only matching are required baselines.

## 8. Architecture

The repository will contain isolated modules with explicit interfaces:

1. `schema`: validated graph, policy, action, and evidence types.
2. `generator`: seeded synthetic architectures and fault injection.
3. `audit`: traversal, residual-path detection, and explanations.
4. `evidence`: artifact-specific validators and receipt verification.
5. `planner`: exact and approximate remediation solvers.
6. `erasetwin`: small-model unlearning audit and matching.
7. `benchmark`: frozen trials, baselines, statistics, and reports.
8. `web`: offline-first visual demonstration consuming exported results.
9. `cli`: reproducible commands for generation, audit, planning, and verification.

The benchmark and core algorithms must not depend on the web interface.

## 9. Experimental design

### 9.1 Benchmark generation

Generate seeded graphs across development and locked holdout partitions. Vary:

- 10 to 10,000 nodes;
- branching factor and pipeline depth;
- artifact-type composition;
- retention constraints;
- action costs;
- single and multiple subjects;
- one or more injected residual faults.

Fault classes include:

- orphaned database record;
- stale biometric template;
- unpurged index entry;
- expired cache still active;
- backup incorrectly marked complete;
- model falsely marked unlearned;
- receipt replay;
- modified receipt;
- receipt bound to the wrong graph root;
- missing or invalid evidence.

The hidden holdout will include topology templates and combinations absent from development.

### 9.2 Baselines

- `receipt-only`: trust a valid signed completion receipt.
- `flat-checklist`: inspect a predefined list of stores without lineage.
- `untyped-traversal`: traverse descendants but apply one generic proof rule.
- `delete-all`: remediate every deletable descendant regardless of cost.
- `random-plan`: random valid action selection.
- `greedy-cover`: deterministic cost-per-newly-covered-artifact baseline.
- `EraseMap`: typed traversal, evidence validation, and exact or approximate planning.

### 9.3 Primary endpoint

False-complete rate:

`number of trials declared COMPLETE while a registered prohibited residual path exists / number of trials with a prohibited residual path`

### 9.4 Secondary endpoints

- fault recall and precision;
- false-alarm rate;
- exact residual-node recall;
- shortest-counterexample correctness;
- proof tamper and replay detection;
- remediation cost and execution-time proxy;
- optimality gap of the approximate planner;
- runtime and peak memory;
- performance by graph size and fault class;
- 95% confidence intervals;
- paired method comparisons on identical trials.

### 9.5 Model-layer experiment

Begin with synthetic identities or Fashion-MNIST-like data and a small model. Compare standard training, exact retraining, and at least two practical unlearning baselines. Development and external holdout data are separated before threshold selection. Multiple independent seeds are required.

CIFAR-10 or a licensed synthetic face dataset may be added only as a frozen external holdout after the small experiment and protocol are working.

## 10. Pre-registered success criteria

The design targets the following criteria; these are not claimed results:

- false-complete rate at or below 5% on the locked graph holdout;
- at least 95% recall of registered residual paths;
- 100% detection of benchmark receipt modifications and replay cases covered by the threat model;
- a lower paired false-complete rate than every baseline;
- at least 20% lower median normalized remediation cost than `delete-all`, without leaving a required artifact uncovered;
- stable conclusions across graph-size strata and independent seeds;
- EraseTwin outperforms random-nonmember auditing against exact retraining on its locked holdout.

Failure to meet a target is reported without changing the endpoint or holdout.

## 11. Statistical protocol

- Freeze generator version, seeds, policy schema, thresholds, and analysis code before holdout execution.
- Report all planned trials, exclusions, and failures.
- Use paired bootstrap confidence intervals for paired method differences.
- Report effect sizes in addition to p-values.
- Correct for multiple secondary comparisons or label them exploratory.
- Keep synthetic and model-layer conclusions separate.
- Never present seeded fault-detection accuracy as evidence of real eGov performance.

## 12. Demonstration design

The offline demo shows one synthetic citizen and a five-branch data map. A deletion request initially removes only the source record. EraseMap highlights the shortest residual path, such as:

`citizen -> face template -> search index -> active identification service`

After applying the proposed remediation plan, the interface shows per-layer states and exports a signed, privacy-minimized receipt with a QR-verifiable payload. The receipt exposes pseudonymous identifiers and commitments, never the underlying biometric sample.

The demo includes three fixed scenarios:

1. false completion caused by an orphaned template;
2. legally retained backup with processing blocked;
3. source records erased but model influence unverified.

## 13. Ethics, safety, and competition compliance

- Do not collect new human biometric data without prior approval required by the relevant SRC/IRB process.
- Prefer fully synthetic identities for the primary benchmark and demonstration.
- Record dataset licenses, provenance, intended use, and removal instructions.
- Do not expose face images, voice samples, government identifiers, or reversible embeddings in receipts.
- Complete required competition forms before any experiment involving people or non-public personal data.
- Maintain a dated research notebook and authorship log.
- Clearly separate student work, mentor guidance, third-party libraries, and AI assistance.

## 14. Novelty claim

The project will not claim to invent data lineage, deletion propagation, signed receipts, certified removal, minimum-cost coverage, or machine unlearning.

The candidate contribution is:

> A typed, cost-aware residual-path audit that joins artifact-specific deletion evidence with paired model-influence evaluation and produces a compact counterexample plus a valid remediation plan.

This claim remains provisional until a systematic prior-art review is completed and documented.

## 15. Deliverables

- versioned open-source repository;
- formal schemas and documented algorithms;
- deterministic synthetic benchmark;
- baseline implementations;
- EraseMap auditor and remediation planner;
- EraseTwin experimental module;
- frozen holdout protocol;
- machine-readable result tables;
- statistical report and plots;

- research paper, poster, abstract, limitations statement, and reproducibility guide.

## 16. Completion gates

The project may be called a working prototype only after unit, integration, and deterministic benchmark tests pass.

It may be called a validated research result only after the locked holdout is executed once under the frozen protocol and all results are reported.

It may be called externally generalizable only after evaluation on an independently sourced topology or dataset not created during development.

It may be described as applicable to government-style systems, but not as validated on eGov or any real government service without authorized external evidence.
